# The Societal Perspective: 3. First Viewpoint Analysis

From a social perspective, the transition from Nokia handsets to the iPhone involves not just the technology itself, but the implications for daily life, so thinking about the transition requires a reflection on what people did with these devices and how things changed for them. The hardware is not as important as the habits that developed on it.

## From a phone to a social tool

The primary reason for the purchase of the initial mobile phones was to call out from the home or office. This is all about to change when text messaging became popular. The first SMS was sent in December 1992 and by 2000, with the launch of the Nokia 3310, texting had become a normal part of life for many, particularly teenagers. They used it to alter plans at the last minute, find out where friends were etc. Meeting times and locations were less important because anyone on the way could be reached.
It also influenced the lifestyle of the family. Additionally, a parent might now text their teen directly, rather than calling the family land line phone number, giving teens some privacy and parents some choice. Both teams received a copy from the same source.

## Phones in the developing world

Things are different in rich countries. Landlines were uncommon in much of sub-Saharan Africa and a mobile phone was often the first telephone to be owned in a household or business. (Suri, Bharadwaj and Jack, 2021) conducted a study on M-Shwari, a digital loan service in Kenya which is accessed through mobile devices. They discovered that such loans enabled households to deal with unexpected circumstances, like a sudden drop in earnings, without reducing their expenditure by as much. Such services evolved from mobile money services like M-Pesa, and many of them operate on cheap, rugged, days-long holding basic phones. That sort of phone did more good to many users than an expensive cellphone.

## The smartphone shift

The iPhone was released in 2007 and the App Store was released in 2008. From then on, the phone was used primarily for calls. It is now a camera, a map, a music player, a bank, a news feed and a social network in one pocket. Since people were taking it with them all day, they were using it throughout the day rather than in brief periods at a desktop.
Some studies estimate that this had no effect on people.Some studies suggest that this had no impact on people. (Allcott et al, 2020) conducted an experiment to make approximately 2,700 users in the United States of America delete their Facebook accounts for four weeks. Those who did not log on online experienced less online time, more time with friends and family, and experienced slightly more overall well-being. (Haidt, 2024) takes it one step further. He believes the transition to the "smartphone childhood" in the early 2010s has hurt the mental health of teens, and that parents and schools should restrict teens' phone usage. But other researchers question the strength of that evidence, and his assertion should be considered a part of a debate. There is little question that communication now became ubiquitous, which altered the way people want to be communicated with by their friends, family, and employers. There is no excuse to be late in your reply now.

## Who was left out

Smartphones were a lot more expensive than the feature phones they replaced, leaving a space between those that can afford one and those that can't. The same gap is becoming important for everyday services, as a smartphone increasingly becomes the norm for banking, ticketing and government forms. Now it also encompasses the capacity of individuals to utilise it and whether it is beneficial to them, and the differences are likely to mirror pre-existing inequalities of income, age and educational attainment. The (GSMA, 2023) comes to the same conclusion for mobile internet. The key challenges are cost, skills and relevance of what is available online, not the lack of coverage, as most people not using it live within range of a network signal. Older people and those living on low incomes are most affected and the same phone is not as effective for all.

## Societal picture

When and where people can be contacted, as well as providing a number of people in poorer areas with their first real access to markets and financial services, have all been transformed by the Nokia era phones. The iPhone and the smartphones that followed did make it firm, and they piled it with a social over-layer, but at the price of greater convenience and information and the loss of attention, privacy and equality of access.
