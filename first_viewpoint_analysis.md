# First Viewpoint Analysis: The Societal Perspective
Looking at the move from Nokia handsets to the iPhone through a societal lens means asking what people did with these devices, and how everyday life changed once they did. The hardware matters less than the habits built around it.
## From a phone to a social tool
Early mobile phones were bought mainly to make calls while away from home or the office. That changed quickly once text messaging caught on. The first SMS was sent in December 1992, and by the time phones like the Nokia 3310 arrived in 2000, texting had become part of daily routine, especially for teenagers. Ling (2004) studied this in Norway and found that young people used mobiles for what he calls micro-coordination: changing plans at the last minute, saying they were running late, working out where friends were. Social life became more flexible. Fixed meeting times and places mattered less because anyone could be reached on the way.
The same research points to a shift in how families worked. A parent could now reach a teenager directly, without going through the household landline, so teenagers gained privacy and parents gained a way to check in. Each side got something from the same device.
## Phones in developing economies
The story looks different outside wealthy countries. In much of sub-Saharan Africa, landline networks barely existed, so mobiles were often the first telephone a household or business ever had. Aker and Mbiti (2010) argue that this lowered the cost of finding information. Traders could compare prices across markets without travelling, and people could contact relatives or services over long distances. They also describe mobile money, such as M-Pesa in Kenya, which let people send payments without a bank account. For many users the basic Nokia-style handset, cheap and tough with a battery that lasted days, did more social good than a high-end smartphone would have.
## The smartphone shift
The iPhone launched in 2007 and the App Store followed in 2008. After that, the phone stopped being mainly a communication device. It became a camera, a map, a music player, a bank, a news feed and a social network in one pocket. Social media moved onto it and, because people carried the phone everywhere, it was used all day instead of in occasional sessions at a desktop.
This changed how people relate to each other. Turkle (2011) argues that constant connection makes people expect more from technology and less from each other, and that being "alone together", physically present but looking at a screen, has become a normal way to spend time with others. Her view is critical, and not everyone accepts it. Ling's earlier work suggests mobiles can strengthen close ties as much as weaken them. Still, the two positions agree on one point: the smartphone made communication constant, and that has changed what people expect of friends, family and employers. Replying late now needs an excuse.
## Who was left out
Access has never been even. Smartphones cost far more than the feature phones they replaced, so a gap opened between people who could afford one and people who could not. Because services such as banking, ticketing and government forms increasingly assume a smartphone, that gap now affects access to ordinary services, which is a bigger problem than owning a slightly older handset. Older people and those on low incomes are the groups most likely to be affected. Aker and Mbiti (2010) also note that benefits depend on network coverage and literacy, so the same phone does not help everyone equally.
## Summary of the societal picture
Nokia-era phones changed when and where people could be reached, and they gave many people in poorer regions their first real connection to markets and services. The iPhone and the smartphones that followed made that connection permanent and added a social layer on top of it, with gains in convenience and information and costs in attention, privacy and fairness of access.
