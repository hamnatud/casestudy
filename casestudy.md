# Evolution of mobile phones to smartphones (Nokia to iPhone)

# Introduction
Mobile technology has undergone a massive evolution from the days of Motorola and Nokia to present day iPhones. The mobile phone market was initially dominated by Nokia in the 90s and early 2000s, with releases such as the Nokia 1110 and 3310 selling hundreds of millions of units across the globe, including in developing countries. These devices were praised for their low price, durability and long battery lives which at the time, was exactly what people wanted in their mobile phone.

However, this all changed when Apple released the original iPhone in 2007. It featured a revolutionary capacitive touchscreen, changing the way people interacted with their phones forever. It also included a multimedia player and a web browser, becoming an all-in-one device that functioned more like the computers and laptops of the time than a mobile phone. Mobile phones have evolved from devices only capable of voice communication and SMS texting to all-in-one computers that have revolutionized the internet and made it an essential part of our lives. Mobile technology is getting more advanced as time goes on, with current flagships being orders of magnitude more capable than the Nokias of the previous decade.

In this case study, we aim to track the history of mobile technology and analyse its development along two viewpoints, from the first mobile phone to present day smartphones. We will analyze the impact that this evolution has had on both general society, such as access to the internet, online shopping and social networking. We will also analyse the impact it has had in the business world, such as improvements to communication in and between companies, the rise of remote work and blurring of work-life boundaries. Finally, we will evaluate the ethical implications that the evolution of mobile technology has had so far and what it may lead to in the future.

# First Viewpoint Analysis: The Societal Perspective
First Viewpoint Analysis: The Societal Perspective
A sociological approach to the shift from Nokia phones to the iPhone would require one to consider what people did with the phones, and how their everyday life changed as a result. It's not about the hardware, it's about the habits that are formed around it.
## From a phone to a social tool
Initially mobile phones were purchased for calling out from home or office. This quickly changed when technology was introduced to send texts. The first SMS was sent in December 1992 and in the year 2000, texting was already a normal part of life for many people, particularly teenagers, with phones such as the Nokia 3310 available. The use of mobiles by young people for what Ling (2004) calls micro-coordination, changing plans at the last minute, saying they were running late, and working out where friends were, was studied in Norway. There was an increase in social freedom. The fixed timing and location of meetings was less important because people were always available to contact on the move. The same study indicates a change in the pattern of family life. Now a parent can communicate directly with a teenager, bypassing the household phone system, thus giving teens privacy and parents a way to monitor. The same device was used to give each side something.
## The phones in the developing economies.
Landline telephones were rarely available in many parts of sub-Saharan Africa and mobile phones were often the first phones a household or business had. According to Aker and Mbiti (2010) this reduced the cost of information acquisition. With the ability to compare prices from market to market without having to move, traders were not required to physically be in the vicinity of a market to make a call to a relative or service. They also provide information on mobile money, including the M-Pesa service in Kenya that enabled individuals to send money without having a bank account. For many, the stripped down Nokia handsets were more socially beneficial, being cheap and rugged with a days' worth of battery life.
## The smartphone shift
In 2007 the iPhone was released, and the App Store in 2008. Following this, the telephone was no longer primarily for communication. It turned into a camera, a map, a music player, a bank, news feed and a social network in one pocket. It became the property of social media and, with people carrying the phone around everywhere, it was used throughout the day rather than in a few sessions on a desktop. This altered the way people interacted with one another. Technology users are increasingly used to more from technology and less from people, and "alone together" (physically present but gazing at a screen) is now a de facto method for spending time together with others, argues Turkle (2011). She's a voice that needs to be heard and not everyone agrees. From Ling's previous research, it seems that mobiles can be used to solidify as well as to dissolve closeness. However, both the roles have a common ground – the smartphone has made communication constant and it has affected what people expects from friends, family and employers. It's time to find an excuse to reply late now.
## Who was left out
The smart phones were far more expensive than the feature phones they replaced, creating a divide between those who were able to afford them and those who were not. That divide now extends to the use of basic services, including banking and ticketing, as well as government forms—more of an issue than owning a slightly older handset. It is most likely to impact older adults and people with low income. Nor is the same phone equally effective in delivering benefits, as Aker and Mbiti (2010) point out, because of network coverage and literacy.
